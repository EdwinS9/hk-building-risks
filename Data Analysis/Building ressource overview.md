## 1. How many buildings need maintenance

Hong Kong has roughly 50,000 buildings, of which about 44,250 are private and around 81% residential. Of the 50,000 buildings, 44,250 are private, with 81 percent being residential buildings.

The maintenance burden concentrates in aged stock. By the surveyors' institute's count, there are nearly 30,000 private buildings 30 years or older — about 60% of the total — and the number over 50 years old has climbed to nearly 10,000 in the past decade. The government separately noted that as at end-2023 there were more than 9,100 buildings aged 50 or above, increasing by about 600 each year.

In scheme terms:

- **MWIS** (windows, buildings 10+ years): there were 27,168 buildings covered as at 31 December 2024.
- **MBIS** (common parts, external walls, projections, signboards, buildings 30+ years): the eligible pool has been cited by government at roughly 15,000–25,000 buildings, and is growing as more buildings cross the 30-year mark.

So the "needs maintenance" universe is on the order of tens of thousands of buildings, with the aged-and-neglected core — including roughly 5,300 "three-nil" buildings with no owners' corporation, residents' organisation, or management company — being the hardest to service.

## 2. How many maintenance actions per year

Two ways to count this:

**Buildings selected annually.** Each year about 2,000 buildings are selected for MBIS and MWIS concurrently, plus a further batch selected for MWIS only. The MWIS-only target has since been tightened to a risk-based selection of around 600 buildings per year.

**Inspection/repair certificates actually filed.** This is the more concrete measure of "actions." Under MWIS in 2024 alone, Qualified Persons submitted to BD roughly 21,759 certificates of prescribed window inspection (where no repair was needed) and 16,741 certificates of prescribed repair — close to 38,500 completed inspection/repair actions in a single year from windows alone, before counting MBIS structural work.

Cumulatively, the scale is large: since MWIS began in 2012, 13,461 target buildings have been selected and 723,219 statutory notices issued; of these, 657,670 had been complied with and 26,647 had not as at end-2024. (Notice counts run high because windows in individual flats can each generate a notice.)

## 3. What an action costs

There are two cost layers — inspection (fixed, modest) and repair (variable, dominant).

**Inspection fees (MBIS).** The surveyors' recommended professional fee scale for a typical residential building inspection runs around HK$50,000 for buildings up to 20 units, HK$50,000–75,000 for 20–50 units, HK$75,000–150,000 for 50–150 units, and HK$150,000–200,000 above that — roughly HK$1,000–2,500 per unit. Window (MWIS) inspections are considerably cheaper.

**Repairs.** This is where most spending occurs, and it varies enormously by building condition — from tens of thousands to several million HK dollars per building for external-wall, structural, and window remediation. There's no single "average bill," but the sector scale is telling: the annual contract value of Hong Kong's Repair, Maintenance, Alteration and Addition (RMAA) sector is about HK$93.1 billion, which is 34.3% of the entire construction industry. BD and the Urban Renewal Authority publish reference unit rates and cost benchmarks precisely because repair costs are so variable and contentious among owners.

## 4. How many people are involved

This spans professionals, contractors, and a large trade workforce:

- **Inspection professionals.** MBIS work is done by Registered Inspectors (RIs) — drawn from authorised persons, registered structural engineers, architects, engineers and surveyors — and MWIS by Qualified Persons (QPs). The eligible professional pool was projected at about 1,800 when the schemes launched; the active RI/QP register today is in the low thousands.
- **Contractors and tradespeople.** Repairs are carried out by Registered General Building Contractors and Minor Works Contractors. There are around 10,000 company registrations across the three minor-works classes and more than 7,000 individual registrations for class III minor works.
- **The wider RMAA workforce** — the painters, scaffolders, plasterers, plumbers and metalworkers doing the physical repairs — numbers in the tens of thousands and is large enough that the Construction Industry Council runs dedicated multi-trade RMAA training certificates to staff it.
- **Government and support side.** BD administers both schemes, supported by the Housing Bureau's Independent Checking Unit (for public-housing-related stock), outsourced consultants who prepare and serve notices, and the URA/Hong Kong Housing Society, which run subsidy schemes (MBISS, Operation Building Bright, elderly-owner grants).

**The structural bottleneck:** the audit watchdog found that of 27,168 MWIS buildings, 14,676 (54%) had never been selected for a notice, and at the 2024 pace of 600 buildings a year it would take about 24 years to cover them — not counting buildings newly crossing the age threshold. So the workforce and selection capacity are the binding constraints, not the size of the building stock itself.

A few caveats: figures come from different years (2023–2025) and different definitions (private vs. all buildings; notices vs. buildings vs. certificates), so the totals aren't perfectly additive. The cleanest, most current numbers are the MWIS ones from the 2025 Audit Commission review; the MBIS and workforce figures are more approximate.
